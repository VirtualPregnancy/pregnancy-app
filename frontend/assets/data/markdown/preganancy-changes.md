During pregnancy your body undergoes a remarkable transformation to accommodate the growth of new life within the uterus. This is orchestrated by a complex interplay of hormonal, physiological and anatomical changes. 

Pregnancy requires your body to adapt to support the growth and development of your baby. This involves changes to the anatomy or structure of key organs like the uterus, changes in the hormones in your body, and changes in how your cardiovascular and immune systems work.

<div class="sub-heading">Physiological changes</div>
From early in pregnancy, your body starts to change how its cardiovascular system works in order to be able to pump more blood to the uterus, and in turn deliver the oxygen and nutrients your baby needs.  By the end of the first trimester, your heart rate will have increased by about 10%, and your blood pressure will have decreased slightly. Over the rest of pregnancy your body will also increase the total amount of blood you have by around a third, which is important for circulation of blood to the placenta, and provides protection against blood that can be lost during childbirth.  As so much new blood is being made, it is really important to make sure that you are getting enough iron in your diet when pregnant, as this helps your blood cells carry oxygen to the placenta.
Your immune system also adapts to pregnancy.  This is important to ‘tolerate’ the placenta which could otherwise be recognised as a foreign organ transplant and attacked by the immune system.  The immune system is not ‘turned down’ though – it still operates well – but rather cells and factors released from your placenta teach it to accept your growing baby. 
Both of these adaptations help to support a healthy pregnancy.

<div class="sub-heading">Hormonal changes</div>
Progesterone and estrogen work together to change your body to support pregnancy.  Progesterone (often called the “pregnancy hormone”) is first produced by the scar left behind in the ovary after it releases the egg (a structure called the corpus luteum), and plays an important role at the very start of pregnancy in maintaining the uterine lining (the endometrium) to support implantation.  The placenta also produces progesterone, and by about 7 weeks of gestation takes over as the main source of this hormone.  As it grows, the placenta continues to produce increasing amounts of progesterone, which has important roles throughout pregnancy in adapting the blood vessels in the uterus to supply blood to the placenta, helping the uterine muscle layer (the myometrium) stretch to accommodate the baby, and in preparing breast tissue to produce milk after birth.
